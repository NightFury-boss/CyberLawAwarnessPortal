# Case Research Master — Cases Where Justice Fell Short

**Status:** Editorially approved with corrections  
**Research baseline:** Phase 1.2 evidence-correction handoff plus subsequent Product Owner review and case-specific research closure notes  
**Prepared:** 8 November 2026

## Editorial methodology

Use the following evidence labels consistently:

- **Court / tribunal finding:** What the court or tribunal expressly decided.
- **Official agency statement:** What a police agency, regulator or government department stated.
- **Party / agency allegation:** An allegation or submission, not a conviction or judicial finding.
- **Secondary reporting:** Context only unless independently verified against the underlying primary record.
- **Open verification:** A document, final outcome or current docket not located.

Never convert an allegation into a fact. Never infer causation from temporal sequence alone. Attribute statistics to their source, cut-off date and definition.

## Case 01 — Sulli Deals & Bulli Bai: Cross-Border Platform Evidence

**Authority:** Delhi Police / courts; connected Supreme Court proceedings  
**Timeline:** Sulli Deals, July 2021; Bulli Bai, January 2022  
**Category:** Investigative & Evidentiary Gaps  
**Classification:** Documented Procedural Shortcoming

### Established narrative
Sulli Deals and Bulli Bai were separate applications using photographs of Muslim women without consent in a derogatory mock-auction format. According to investigating authorities, the two incidents occurred at different times and involved separately accused individuals with distinct alleged roles; arrests or investigative allegations do not by themselves establish judicial guilt unless proved at trial. Official MHA parliamentary material supports police action and the Sulli Deals investigation. Contemporary reporting described difficulty obtaining subscriber information from GitHub through domestic process and subsequent pursuit of formal international legal assistance.

GitHub's 2021 Transparency Report supports the general proposition that foreign-government requests for user information are routed through the US Department of Justice and appropriate international legal process. It does not establish the precise date or exact response in either case.

The Supreme Court order of 1 December 2025 in *Aumkareshwar Thakur v. State of NCT of Delhi*, W.P. (Crl.) 27/2022 and connected matter, disposed of the petitions in light of subsequent developments and liberty to pursue remedies in accordance with law. Do not interpret this order as proof that every underlying criminal trial or investigation concluded.

### Publication safeguards
- Separate incident chronologies and perpetrators.
- Do not state that delay in Sulli Deals caused Bulli Bai.
- Use qualified wording for the GitHub / MLAT sequence.
- Do not claim a case-specific GitHub refusal without the underlying correspondence.
- Obtain primary copies of 2026 magistrate orders before describing their detailed reasoning.
- Protect complainant privacy and preserve the distinction between allegations and judicial findings.

### Learning point
Cross-border platform evidence can require formal international legal process. A domestic notice does not necessarily compel a foreign service provider to disclose user information.

## Case 02 — Jaiprakash Kulkarni & Anr. v. Banking Ombudsman

**Court:** Bombay High Court, W.P. 1150/2023  
**Date:** 13 June 2024  
**Neutral citation:** `2024:BHC-OS:8627-DB`  
**Category:** Banking & Regulatory Inquiries  
**Classification:** Established Institutional Failure

### Established narrative
Unauthorized RTGS/NEFT transfers were made from the corporate current account of Pharma Search Ayurveda Pvt. Ltd. at Bank of Baroda on 1–2 October 2022. The High Court ordered refund of ₹76,90,017 with 6% annual interest from 2 October 2022 until payment. Cyber Cell transaction records separately reflected ₹76,90,444.70; do not conflate these figures.

The Cyber Cell material distinguished beneficiary-addition notifications from transaction alerts. Beneficiary-addition notifications on 1 October were not delivered to the registered mobile/email. On 2 October transaction / OTP messages were sent to the registered mobile number, while relevant bank emails were not received at the registered email.

The Court quashed the Ombudsman decision because the inquiry did not properly examine whether relevant alerts were delivered. It applied the RBI customer-protection framework and found the Ombudsman's reasoning inadequate.

### Publication safeguards
- Correct neutral citation to `2024:BHC-OS:8627-DB`.
- Do not say simply “OTP never arrived”.
- Distinguish amount ordered refunded from the Cyber Cell transaction total.
- Do not overstate the judgment as a universal rule that any 2FA failure automatically establishes bank liability.

### Learning point
Generation of an authentication message is not the same as successful delivery. Evidence of delivery, account controls and customer conduct must be examined rather than assumed.

## Case 03 — Cyberabad Instant Micro-Lending Investigations

**Authorities:** Telangana Police, Telangana High Court, Enforcement Directorate, RBI, Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA  
**Timeline:** 2020–2026  
**Category:** Platform & Marketplace Lags  
**Classification:** Established Institutional Failure, narrowly limited to NBFC-fintech arrangements adjudicated in attachment proceedings

### Established record
The Telangana High Court's W.P. (PIL) 13/2021 was disposed of on 9 November 2021 after considering a DGP affidavit describing police actions. The affidavit referred to 290 loan-shark apps identified on Google Play, requests to block them, 66 criminal cases across Telangana, arrests connected with identified companies, and chargesheets in certain Hyderabad Cyber Crime cases. Attribute these figures and actions to the affidavit; the Court's disposal was not a finding that all investigations were complete.

In *Wecash Technology Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Telangana*, Criminal Petition 3588/2021, 5 July 2021, the High Court recorded six police case references. Some cited IPC Section 306; others included CrPC Section 174. These entries are not proof of six suicides or of legal causation.

The ED announced on 23 August 2024 that it had filed a prosecution complaint arising from 43 FIRs registered in 2020–21 by Cyberabad and Rachakonda police; the Special MSJ Court, Nampally, took cognizance on 22 August 2024. The ED alleged coercive lending and misuse of borrowers' contacts, images and personal information. Treat these as allegations in proceedings, not convictions. Do not combine this 43-FIR investigation with the separate ED release concerning 118 FIRs and 242 apps.

In *M/s Smartcoin Financials Pvt. Ltd. v. Deputy Director*, Criminal Petition 2090/2023, 6 November 2024, the Telangana High Court quashed PMLA proceedings against the specific petitioner in the circumstances of that record, including the absence of a predicate offence against it in the police charge sheet. This did not exonerate the sector.

In *M/s Krazybee Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Directorate of Enforcement*, Criminal Petition 5885/2023, 11 March 2025, the High Court quashed ECIR proceedings against the named petitioners in relation to the FIRs and charge-sheet position before it. This is also petitioner-specific.

On 5 January 2026, the Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA dismissed appeals by Pawan Finvest, Uu Cash Technologies, BCL Enterprises and Care India Finvest and upheld the challenged provisional attachment decisions. The Tribunal examined the NBFC-fintech arrangements before it and found outsourcing of core regulated activities inconsistent with RBI requirements. These are attachment proceedings, not criminal convictions of every company or a blanket sector finding.

### Regulatory timeline
- RBI 2022 Digital Lending Guidelines: historical framework.
- RBI Digital Lending Directions, 2025: issued 8 May 2025; current framework for relevant later conduct.
- Preserve historical legal analysis for conduct in 2020–2022; do not retroactively apply 2025 rules.

### Publication safeguards
- No unverified aggregate death count.
- No legal-causation assertion for individual deaths without case-specific adjudication.
- Attribute police, ED and Tribunal statements precisely.
- Do not claim police negligence solely from regulatory gaps or multiple investigations.
- Keep Smartcoin and Krazybee holdings limited to the named petitioners.
- Distinguish provisional attachment from conviction and confiscation.

### Learning point
A regulated lender cannot shed its regulatory responsibility by outsourcing core lending functions to a fintech provider. Borrower-data access, collection practices and recovery communications require oversight and lawful controls.

## Case 04 — Harshada Bindu Dholepatil v. Vodafone Idea Ltd.

**Tribunal:** Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal  
**Case:** Cyber Appeal 7/2019  
**Date:** 25 September 2024  
**Category:** Banking & Regulatory Inquiries  
**Classification:** Established Institutional Failure

### Established narrative
A duplicate SIM application was made on 13 January 2014 at an Idea Cellular franchisee in Pune. The identity documents did not match the original subscriber's details and the original SIM stopped working. Unauthorized NEFT transfers of ₹1.5 lakh each, total ₹3 lakh, followed.

TDSAT dismissed Vodafone Idea's appeal and upheld compensation of ₹3,00,000 plus 12% compound interest, as specified in the order.

### Publication safeguards
- Call this a TDSAT judgment, not a High Court judgment.
- Explain the identity-document mismatch, not merely duplicate SIM issuance.
- Describe the event as SIM-swap-enabled financial fraud.
- Do not claim TDSAT established every technical detail of OTP interception.
- Separate telecom compensation liability from criminal investigation and bank liability.
- Apply law as it stood in 2014 when discussing historical conduct.

### Learning point
Subscriber identity verification is a critical control. A replacement SIM can become a gateway to account takeover when identity checks fail.

## Case 05 — AePS Biometric Cloned-Deed Exploits

**Authorities:** Hyderabad Police, Haryana Revenue & Disaster Management Department, MHA, RBI  
**Timeline:** 2023–2026  
**Category:** Platform & Marketplace Lags  
**Classification:** Documented Procedural Shortcoming

### Established narrative
Hyderabad Police's 22 November 2023 press note described an alleged operation involving land-registration files and cloned fingerprints. Police reported approximately 2,500 files, approximately 1,000 cloned fingerprints, approximately 2,500 AePS transactions, approximately ₹10 lakh loss, six apprehensions and ₹5 lakh returned at that time. Attribute all figures to police.

A Haryana Government memorandum acknowledged that Web HALRIS registered deeds made scanned fingerprints, Aadhaar details and signatures publicly accessible and directed masking/removal.

MHA parliamentary material reported approximately 29,000 AePS fraud incidents to NCRP; NCRB did not separately count biometric cloning as a category. Public exposure and spoofing do not establish a breach of UIDAI's Central Identities Data Repository.

NPCI committee material from February 2024 described dual/face-authentication development as ongoing. Do not assert a universal liveness-detection requirement effective 1 January 2024.

RBI AePS Touchpoint Operator Due Diligence Directions, 2025 were issued 27 June 2025 and became effective 1 January 2026.

### Publication safeguards
- Attribute incident figures to Hyderabad Police.
- Distinguish exposed registry documents from central Aadhaar database compromise.
- Remove unsupported NPCI liveness-detection date.
- Explain regulatory developments with issue and effective dates.

### Learning point
Biometric data, once exposed in public documents, cannot be reset like a password. Data minimization, masking and stronger authentication are essential.

## Case 06 — AIIMS New Delhi Ransomware Incident

**Authorities:** Ministry of Health & Family Welfare; MeitY / CERT-In; Lok Sabha  
**Date:** November 2022  
**Category:** Critical Infrastructure & Complex Attribution  
**Classification:** Unresolved Incident

### Established narrative
MoHFW Lok Sabha UQ 1837 (16 December 2022) stated that five physical NIC e-Hospital servers were affected, the backup server was unaffected, and data was restored to new servers. Services were restored over approximately two weeks. Manual procedures were used for registration, appointments, admissions/discharges, surgeries and records during the disruption.

MeitY Lok Sabha UQ 2310 (21 December 2022) stated that preliminary CERT-In analysis found unknown threat actors had compromised servers and that improper network segmentation contributed to the disruption. The government reported an FIR and seizure of servers.

The approximately 1.3 TB figure refers to encrypted data in the official account. Encryption is not proof of exfiltration. The Government stated that no specific ransom amount was demanded; remove the unsupported ₹200 crore claim.

### Publication safeguards
- Distinguish encryption from exfiltration.
- Do not claim data theft without evidence.
- Do not attribute the attack to China or a state actor.
- Include backup restoration, manual operations and recovery measures.
- Attribute improper segmentation to the MeitY parliamentary response.

### Learning point
Network segmentation, resilient backups and continuity procedures are critical for healthcare infrastructure. A service outage can harm patients even where clinical control systems are not compromised.

## Case 07 — Pegasus Technical Committee Inquiry

**Court:** Supreme Court of India  
**Case:** *Manohar Lal Sharma v. Union of India*, W.P. (Crl.) 314/2021 and connected petitions  
**Category:** Critical Infrastructure & Complex Attribution  
**Classification:** Documented Procedural Shortcoming

### Established narrative
On 27 October 2021, the Supreme Court constituted an independent technical committee supervised by former Supreme Court judge Justice R.V. Raveendran. The Court criticized the Union Government's limited affidavit as inadequate to address the allegations and emphasized that national security is not a blanket exemption from judicial scrutiny.

On 25 August 2022, the committee and overseeing judge submitted reports in sealed covers. Public proceedings described 29 devices examined and malware-related findings on five, but the Court could not conclude that the malware was Pegasus. The Court publicly reported the committee's observation that the Government had not cooperated / had not provided specific responses to its technical questionnaire. Attribute this carefully.

The full reports remained sealed in the Supreme Court's custody in the records reviewed. Leaked phone-number lists indicate potential targets, not confirmed infections.

Later listings in 2025 and 2026 were located, but no final disposal order was verified in the research reviewed.

### Publication safeguards
- Do not equate malware with Pegasus.
- Do not equate a leaked target list with confirmed infection.
- Attribute non-cooperation to the committee report and Court's public observations.
- Do not infer Government deployment.
- Do not invent sealed report contents.
- Retain the latest docket / final-disposal caveat pending verification.

### Learning point
Independent forensic scrutiny and transparent judicial oversight are essential where surveillance allegations implicate privacy and national security.

## Case 08 — Ravinder Singh @ Kaku v. State of Punjab

**Court:** Supreme Court of India  
**Case:** Criminal Appeal 1307/2019 and connected appeals  
**Date:** 4 May 2022  
**Citation:** `2022 INSC 505`; `(2022) 7 SCC 581`  
**Category:** Investigative & Evidentiary Gaps  
**Classification:** Documented Procedural Shortcoming

### Established narrative
The prosecution case concerned kidnapping and murder of two children in Moga, Punjab, in September 2009. The Supreme Court set aside Ravinder Singh's convictions under IPC Sections 302 and 364 and directed release unless required in another case.

The prosecution relied on circumstantial evidence, including call-detail records. The Supreme Court found multiple weaknesses: motive was not established, last-seen witness accounts were inconsistent, arrest and recovery evidence was contradictory, school-bag recovery evidence was problematic, and the circumstantial chain was incomplete.

The CDRs lacked the certificate required under Section 65B(4) of the Indian Evidence Act for the secondary electronic records relied upon. The Court did not acquit solely because of the missing certificate.

### Current law
- Historical case: Indian Evidence Act Sections 65A and 65B.
- Current law: BSA Sections 62 and 63, effective 1 July 2024.
- Include Section 63(4) and the Schedule's two-part certificate.
- Explain the original-device exception under the *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar* interpretation of Section 65B.
- Explain BSA Section 170(2) transitional protection for proceedings pending immediately before commencement.
- Distinguish admissibility from evidentiary weight.

### Publication safeguards
Do not say all electronic evidence always requires a certificate regardless of production method. Do not say the missing certificate alone caused acquittal or proved innocence.

### Learning point
A digital record must satisfy applicable admissibility rules, but admissibility alone does not prove motive, identity or guilt.

## Case 09 — CFCFRMS / 1930 Cyber-Fraud Restitution Gap

**Authorities:** Parliamentary Standing Committee on Communications & IT; MHA / I4C; India Code  
**Category:** Banking & Regulatory Inquiries  
**Classification:** Documented Procedural Shortcoming

### Historical baseline
The 54th Report of the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Communications and Information Technology (February 2024) recorded CFCFRMS figures as of 31 October 2023:

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---:|
| Complaints | 16,28,357 |
| Cases with money put on hold | 3,87,987 |
| Cases in which money restored | 1,207 |
| Amount reported lost | ₹8,586.71 crore |
| Amount lien-marked | ₹878.87 crore |
| Amount returned | ₹4.15 crore |

These categories are not interchangeable.

### Later official developments
- MHA reported more than ₹7,130 crore saved across more than 23.02 lakh complaints (2 December 2025).
- MHA parliamentary response in February 2026 described the national SOP issued 2 January 2026.
- MHA reported more than ₹8,690 crore saved across more than 24.65 lakh complaints as of 31 January 2026.
- MHA's 22 July 2026 update reported more than ₹11,158 crore saved across more than 32.80 lakh complaints by 30 June 2026.
- The same July 2026 update separately reported, for FY 2023–24 through FY 2025–26, more than ₹9,079 crore lien-marked and more than ₹206 crore refunded. These are distinct measures and periods.
- Money Restoration Module and Grievance Redressal Module became functional from April 2026.
- The official Money Restoration Portal provides an online restoration request process using a 14-digit NCRP acknowledgement ID; a request is not a guaranteed refund.
- Full text of the 2 January 2026 SOP was not located in the research reviewed.

### Legal framework
- CrPC Section 451 → BNSS Section 497.
- CrPC Section 457 → BNSS Section 503.
- BNSS Section 107 is a separate attachment, forfeiture and restoration mechanism.
- BNSS Section 531(2)(a) preserves the CrPC for specified proceedings already pending immediately before commencement.
- Do not imply a universal Magistrate-order requirement for every hold or automatic entitlement to restitution.

### Publication safeguards
- Exclude unverified Lok Sabha UQ 2577 (6 August 2024) / ₹11,000 crore attribution.
- Never label “saved”, “held”, “frozen” or “lien-marked” as “refunded”.
- Attach each statistic to its source date, period and definition.

### Learning point
Stopping the movement of stolen money is only the first stage. Victims also need an accessible, lawful and timely restoration process.

## Case 10 — Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant Administrative Network Malware

**Authorities:** NPCIL, DAE, CERT-In and parliamentary records  
**Timeline:** Detection reported 4 September 2019; public communications 29–30 October 2019  
**Category:** Critical Infrastructure & Complex Attribution  
**Classification:** Documented Procedural Shortcoming

### Established narrative
CERT-In notified DAE/NPCIL on 4 September 2019. The investigation described an infected PC belonging to an Internet-connected administrative network at Kudankulam. DAE/NPCIL public communication on 30 October 2019 confirmed the infection while stating that critical plant systems were not affected.

The 29 October KKNPP communication denied reports of an attack on control systems; the 30 October NPCIL statement confirmed malware on an administrative PC. Explain the different network environments rather than saying the reactor was hacked.

Official parliamentary references include Lok Sabha UQ 659 (20 November 2019), Rajya Sabha Questions 481 and 483 (21 November 2019), DAE reply (27 November 2019) and Rajya Sabha Starred Question 109 (28 November 2019). Do not rely on the incorrect earlier reference to Lok Sabha UQ 1297.

2019 measures included administrative-network checks, hardening Internet/intranet connectivity, restricting removable media and blocking malicious websites/IPs. Broader DAE measures described in 2022 must be dated and scoped; do not imply every measure was installed at KKNPP immediately after the 2019 incident.

### Publication safeguards
- Distinguish administrative IT from isolated plant control/instrumentation systems.
- State that Government reported no impact on critical plant systems.
- Do not present Dtrack, Lazarus attribution, exfiltration or operational disruption as official findings unless the cited primary record establishes them.
- Focus the procedural shortcoming on public communication clarity, not an unproven reactor compromise.

### Learning point
Critical infrastructure security depends on separation of administrative IT and operational technology, accurate incident communication and careful public explanation of what was and was not affected.
